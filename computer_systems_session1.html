From Sand to Silicon: How Chips Are Made

Ordinary sand contains silica (SiO₂). After extreme purification, it becomes silicon — the semiconductor material behind modern processors.

1. PurifySand is refined into an ultra-pure silicon crystal.
2. SliceThe crystal is cut and polished into thin circular wafers.
3. Print billions of circuitsPhotolithography, deposition and etching build microscopic transistor layers.

The process repeats many times before each wafer is cut into individual chips, tested and packaged.

01
PURE SILICONSilica is purified into electronic-grade silicon.
02
CRYSTAL & WAFERA single crystal is grown, sliced and mirror-polished.
03
PHOTORESISTA light-sensitive coating is applied to the wafer.
04
UV LITHOGRAPHYA mask projects microscopic circuit patterns.
05
ETCH & BUILDLayers are etched, deposited and repeated many times.
06
DICE & PACKAGEFinished dies are tested, cut and packaged as chips.

What Is a Computer, Really?

Input — takes data in
Processing — works on the data
Storage — saves data
Output — gives results back

At its core, a computer only understands two states: ON and OFF — represented as 1 and 0. Everything else (games, this presentation, ChatGPT) is layers built on top of that simple idea.

Why binary and not our normal 10 digits? Because electricity is easiest to control in two clean states — on or off — not ten different voltage levels.

The Input → Process → Output Cycle

INPUT  →  PROCESS  →  OUTPUT  ↔  STORAGE

Example: Typing a message (input) → phone processes keystrokes (process) → text appears on screen (output) → message saved in chat history (storage)

Gen 1 · 1940s–1956

Gen 1 — Vacuum Tubes

  • Machines like ENIAC filled entire rooms and weighed tons
  • Used vacuum tubes (like old light bulbs) as switches
  • Huge, power-hungry, and generated tons of heat
  • Tubes burned out constantly — literally had "bugs" (moths got stuck inside, giving us the term "debugging")
Gen 2 · 1956–1963

Gen 2 — Transistors (silicon enters here)

  • The transistor replaced the vacuum tube: smaller, faster, far less heat, more reliable
  • This is the moment silicon becomes the hero of the story
  • Transistors are made from silicon — a semiconductor, a material that can be controlled to either conduct or block electricity, ideal for an on/off switch
  • Computers shrink from room-sized to closet-sized
Gen 3 · 1964–1971

Gen 3 — Integrated Circuits

  • Instead of wiring individual transistors together, engineers found a way to print thousands of them onto a single silicon chip
  • This is the real birth of "the chip"
  • Smaller, faster, cheaper, and mass-producible
Gen 4 · 1971–Today

Gen 4 — Microprocessors

  • Entire CPUs are now printed on one chip (Intel's 4004, 1971)
  • This triggers the personal computer revolution — computers small and cheap enough for homes and offices
  • Modern chips pack billions of transistors onto something smaller than a fingernail
Gen 5 · Now & Emerging

Gen 5 — Now & Emerging (AI Era)

  • Computers move from simply following instructions to learning patterns and generating content.
  • AI accelerators and GPUs process thousands of operations in parallel.
  • Cloud platforms make powerful AI available from almost any device.
  • Quantum computing may shape the next generation of problem-solving.

Moore's Law: The Pace of the Journey

In 1965, engineer Gordon Moore observed that the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubles every two years.

1971
Intel 4004
~2,300 transistors
1996
Pentium Pro
~5.5 million transistors
Today
Modern CPUs/GPUs
Tens of billions of transistors

That's why your phone is more powerful than room-sized computers from decades ago.
